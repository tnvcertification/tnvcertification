  - type: content-section
    id: 0011-why-documented-information-needs-control
    title: Why documented information needs control
    description: Documents can support decisions, process execution, quality checks, and organizational learning. When the status of information is unclear, people may follow outdated instructions, miss requirements, or struggle to retrieve evidence. Control does not mean every activity needs a lengthy procedure. Organizations should determine what information is necessary for effective process operation, taking into account their size, activity complexity, process interactions, risks, and people’s competence.

  - type: content-section
    id: 0011-six-practical-controls
    title: Six practical controls
    items:
      - title: 1. Clear identification
        description: Give information identifiers that help users recognize what they need. These may include a title, reference code, date, owner, status, and revision where appropriate. Use a consistent naming approach that remains easy to understand.
      - title: 2. Review and approval
        description: Ensure information is reviewed by appropriate people before use. Approval should indicate that its content has been assessed for its intended purpose. For documents affecting quality or safety, define reviewers with appropriate competence and authority.
      - title: 3. Distribution, access, and use
        description: Users should be able to locate relevant information when needed. Define storage locations, permissions, distribution methods, and how obsolete versions are withdrawn or marked to prevent unintended use. Not everyone needs permission to edit documents.
      - title: 4. Change control
        description: Changes should be traceable to an appropriate level. The organization may define who proposes, reviews, approves, and implements changes; what changed; and how affected users are informed. Controls should be proportionate to the document's purpose and risk.
      - title: 5. Storage and protection
        description: Keep information legible, retrievable, and protected from loss, damage, unauthorized changes, or disclosure. For digital information, consider backups, role-based access, device security, and recovery. For physical records, consider storage conditions and damage prevention.
      - title: 6. Retention and disposition
        description: Define how long records need to be kept and how they will be disposed of when retention ends. Consider process needs, contracts, laws, and other obligations. Avoid destroying evidence still required or retaining sensitive information without a reason.
  - type: content-section
    id: 0011-a-simple-example-product-inspection-instructions
    title: "A simple example: product inspection instructions"
    description: Imagine an inspection team using product inspection instructions. The instructions should have clear identification and status, be approved before use, be available at the point of work, and be revised when the inspection method changes. When a new revision is issued, the organization should make sure users understand the change and that the previous version is no longer used as the active instruction. Completed inspection forms, meanwhile, should be retained as evidence according to the defined retention rules. This shows that document and record control are not merely administrative activities. They support consistent process execution and help the organization trace what happened.

  - type: content-section
    id: 0011-conclusion
    title: Conclusion
    description: Effective ISO 9001 document control is not about the number of procedures or folders. It is about ensuring the information people need is available, suitable, protected, and managed throughout its lifecycle. Start with critical processes, understand users’ needs, and apply simple, consistent controls. Documented information management must be tailored to the applicable edition of the standard, organizational context, and relevant legal, contractual, and stakeholder requirements. This article is general guidance, not an official interpretation of the standard or a guarantee of audit or certification outcomes.
